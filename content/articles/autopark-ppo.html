PPO: learning without overcorrecting.

One section from Auto Park

I used Proximal Policy Optimization, or PPO, to train a model called a policy to choose throttle and steering in the parking experiments. I gave it the car’s state and a target directly from PyBullet, then used the drives it collected to update the model.

I scored what the car did through rewards from the environment and combined those over time into a return, giving later rewards less weight. Even when a maneuver earned a good return, making its actions much more likely after one batch could make the next drives worse. I used PPO-Clip to limit how much extra credit the objective gave that kind of increase.

What gets updated?

I sampled actions from the policy during training, then compared how likely the new and old policies made the same action in the same state. I used their ratio, r, which starts at 1 before the update, rises when the action becomes more likely, and falls when it becomes less likely. Because throttle and steering are continuous, I was comparing probability densities.

I also needed an advantage estimate to judge whether the action led to a better or worse outcome than expected from that state. I used its sign to decide whether the update should make that action more likely or less likely.

I collected drives with the old policy and updated the model using smaller groups of those samples, called minibatches, while keeping their stored log-likelihoods fixed. Subtracting those log-likelihoods and taking exp gave me the new likelihood divided by the old one.

I used minimum to take the smaller contribution for each sample, then averaged those contributions across the batch. I used the minus sign to turn the objective into a loss I could minimize. I’ve used an illustrative ε = 0.2 for the bounds here.

I’ve only shown the policy update in this snippet. In the full PPO implementation, I also needed advantage estimates and a trained value function, and could reuse a rollout across several optimization passes. I used the conceptual helper joint_log_prob for the log-likelihood of the whole throttle-and-steering pair, summing across the action dimensions when they’re modeled independently.

One control decision

To work through one update, I can take a sampled throttle-and-steering action with A = +1. If the new policy makes it 1.5 times as likely, the raw contribution becomes 1.5, while the clipped contribution stays at 1.2. I use the smaller value, 1.2, in the objective.

I checked the other direction with A = −1 and r = 0.5, which gives min(−0.5, −0.8) = −0.8. That stops me gaining more from this sample just by making the bad action even less likely, while still penalizing a change that makes it more likely.

What clipping doesn’t promise

I could still end up with ratios outside the clipping bounds, because clipping changed the objective’s incentive without enforcing a hard boundary on the policy. I handled the car’s steering limits separately in the environment.

I had to keep that distinction in mind when reading a parking result: making the training update more conservative didn’t prove the car could handle a new path. I still needed to evaluate it in situations it hadn’t practiced.

I used Stable-Baselines3’s PPO implementation for these experiments, and I’ve kept the pseudocode focused on its central objective so the update is easier to follow.
